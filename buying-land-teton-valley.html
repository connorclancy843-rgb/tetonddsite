Where you're buying: Teton Valley, Idaho.

Teton Valley sits in Teton County, Idaho, on the west slope of the Tetons — the quieter, more affordable side of the same range that faces Jackson Hole. The valley's towns are Victor at the base of Teton Pass, Driggs the county seat in the middle, and Tetonia at the open north end. Alta rounds it out on the Wyoming side, reached by road only through Idaho. Land ranges from in-town townsite lots to subdivision parcels to open rural acreage, and the rules that apply shift as you move between them.

The single most useful thing to understand up front: a parcel being listed for sale tells you nothing about whether it's practical to build a home on. Everything below is what closes that gap.

The checklist

Five things that decide whether a lot is buildable.

01 · Access

Can you legally reach it, year-round?

Confirm legal access — a deeded road or recorded easement, not just a two-track — and think about winter maintenance. A landlocked or seasonally impassable parcel is a very different proposition.

02 · Water & sewer

City services, or well and septic?

Townsite lots may tie into municipal water and sewer; many subdivision and rural lots need a permitted well and a septic system approved through Eastern Idaho Public Health. This drives real cost and timeline.

03 · Zoning & use

Does the zoning allow the home you want?

Teton County, Idaho and the towns set what can be built and how big. Setbacks, height and density all live here. Alta parcels answer to Teton County, Wyoming instead.

04 · The site itself

Is there a clean building envelope?

Floodplain, wetlands, steep slope, utility easements and irrigation ditches can all shrink or block where a house can actually go. A survey and a look at the maps beat an optimistic guess.

05 · Covenants

What do the CC&Rs require?

In subdivisions and communities like Teton Springs, covenants and design review can dictate size, style, materials and even a deadline to finish building. Read them before you sign.

Land vs. a finished home

Buying dirt is the cheap part. Building is the work.

Raw land carries the lowest sticker price, but it also carries everything after it: design, engineering, permitting, a contractor, a construction budget, and the risk if costs or the market move mid-build. In this valley the genuinely scarce thing isn't land — it's finished, well-built homes ready to move into. That's the whole reason our model exists.

So there are three honest paths. Buy raw land and run the build yourself. Buy a finished home and skip all of it — see our new-construction homes. Or, if you already own a lot, partner it into a build with us so it becomes a finished home without you fronting capital or managing a contractor — that's our landowner program. And if you want a builder to construct a custom home on land you intend to keep, that's a build-for-hire job our sister company Artistic Builders handles.

Common questions

Buying-land FAQ

What makes a lot in Teton Valley buildable?

Broadly: legal access, the ability to get water and sewer or a permitted well and septic, zoning that allows a home, and no physical or regulatory constraint — wetlands, floodplain, steep slope, easements — that blocks a building envelope. A parcel can be for sale and still not be practical to build on, which is why each of these is worth confirming before you buy.

Do most Teton Valley lots use a well and septic or city services?

It depends on location. Lots inside the townsites of Victor, Driggs and Tetonia may have access to municipal water and sewer, while many subdivision and rural lots rely on a private well and a septic system permitted through Eastern Idaho Public Health. Rural parcels, including much of Tetonia and Alta, are typically well and septic.

Does buying in an HOA or subdivision change things?

Yes. Many Teton Valley lots sit in subdivisions or communities like Teton Springs with covenants, architectural review and sometimes required design guidelines or build timelines. Read the CC&Rs before buying — they can affect what, how large and when you can build.

Who handles zoning and permits in Teton County, Idaho?

Teton County, Idaho and the individual towns handle land use and building permits, with the county offices in Driggs. Septic permits run through Eastern Idaho Public Health. Alta parcels fall under Teton County, Wyoming instead, even though they're reached through Idaho.

Should I buy raw land or a finished home?

Raw land is cheaper up front but you carry the time, cost and risk of designing, permitting and building — and finished, well-built homes are the scarce part of this market. If you want the end result without running a build, buying new construction, or partnering your lot into a build, can make more sense than doing it yourself.

Can Teton Development & Design build on a lot I buy?

We're a developer, not a build-for-hire contractor — we build on land we own or partner on and sell the finished homes. If you own a lot and want to turn it into a home without selling it, our landowner partnership may fit. If you want a builder to construct a custom home on land you'll keep, our sister company Artistic Builders handles custom builds for hire.
